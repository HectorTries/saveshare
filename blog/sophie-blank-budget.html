Pay Yourself First

How Sophie Blank spends her month.

By Tori | August 2026
Monthly wealth allocations
£3,466
ISA · Cash · Pension

"If you prioritise future you on the first of the month, you can spend guilt-free for the rest of the month."

Sophie Blank used to dread payday. She'd use this month's salary to pay off last month's credit card bill — the maths of paying twenty-something percent interest to borrow from next month's salary is the kind of slow-motion financial disaster most people never quite escape. Sophie escaped. Now, she says, payday is her favourite day of the month.

The transformation, in her own telling, comes down to a single shift: pay yourself first. "The smallest shift with the biggest impact," she calls it. The mechanics are simple. On day one of the month, before any bill is paid, before any subscription renews, before any coffee is bought, the money that builds long-term wealth goes out the door. Then, and only then, does the rest of the month happen.

The Buckets

Here's what "pay yourself first" looks like in Sophie's month. She walked through it line by line in a recent video:

  1. £1,366 — stocks and shares ISA. The main event. The long-term, capital-growth engine. This is the number that compounds.
  2. £300 — cash ISA, for emergencies. The buffer. The thing that stops you reaching for a credit card when the boiler dies.
  3. £1,800 — pension. Straight in. The most important line on the page, and the one most twenty-eight-year-olds aren't writing yet.
  4. £1,500 — fixed costs. Rent, bills, the non-negotiables. Less than 30% of her monthly income, which means the rest of the month has somewhere to go.
  5. Sinking funds. The category she's evangelical about. Sinking funds are pots of money, ring-fenced in advance, for known future costs — a holiday, a car service, Christmas, a new laptop. You name it, she has a sinking fund for it.
  6. £350 a week — spending. The guilt-free allowance. The bit that's allowed to be fun.

Add those first three lines up — £1,366 plus £300 plus £1,800 is £3,466 a month — and Sophie's monthly wealth allocation is most people's monthly salary. The pension line alone is more than the UK median full-time wage. It's a serious machine, and it runs every month whether she feels like it or not.

Sinking Funds

The line that tells you the most about Sophie isn't the £1,800 pension. It's the bit about the sinking funds. Most people budget by category — a £400 monthly food shop, a £150 leisure budget, a £60 streaming pile. Sophie doesn't budget. She allocates, into named pots, ahead of time, and then lets the rest of the month be the rest of the month.

The reason this matters is psychological. A budget is a ceiling. An allocation is a permission slip. When you know the holiday pot has £200 in it and the Christmas pot has £150, you don't have to feel guilty about the £350 weekly spending — it's already accounted for. The decision was made on day one. The rest of the month is execution, not negotiation.

The £350

"I don't like to budget," Sophie says, "but I like to set around £350 a week for spending. Cause that way you can spend guilt free while also making sure that future you is sorted out."

It's a small number, £350, but it does a lot of work. It is, in effect, the budget for everything that isn't a fixed cost or a savings goal. Coffee. Lunch. Clothes. The cinema. The book you saw on TikTok. Whatever. The £350 is the place where Sophie gets to be a person, not a financial plan.

"If you prioritise future you on the first of the month, you can spend guilt-free for the rest of the month."

The transformation isn't really about the £350, though. It's about the £1,366 plus the £1,800 that went out before the £350 was ever touched. The £350 is what you can spend when you've already paid yourself. The discipline came first. The fun came second. In that order.
